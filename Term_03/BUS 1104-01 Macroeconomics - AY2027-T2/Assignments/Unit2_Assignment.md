# Unemployment and Inflation in Bangladesh, 2016–2025

Bangladesh reports low unemployment but high inflation. This report reviews both over the past decade and compares the implications with a developed country.

## What Is the Current Unemployment Rate, and How Is It Measured?

The Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) Labour Force Survey puts 2024 unemployment at 3.66%, and the International Labour Organization (ILO) estimates 3.78% for 2025 (ILO, 2025; TBS Report, 2025). A person counts as unemployed after working less than one hour in the past seven days, being available for work, and searching for paid work in the past 30 days (TBS Report, 2025). Economists compute the rate as the unemployed divided by the labor force (employed plus unemployed people aged 15 or older), times 100 (Shapiro et al., 2023). An ILO recalculation for 2024 divides 2.61 million unemployed by a 71.68 million labor force, giving 3.64% (Kapoor, 2026). Discouraged and underemployed workers escape this count, which Shapiro et al. (2023) call hidden unemployment.

As Table 1 shows, the rate stayed between 4.35% and 4.50% through 2019 even though GDP grew above 6.5% a year, because employment rose only 0.36% for each 1% of growth between 2016 and 2022 (Kapoor, 2026). COVID-19 then lifted it to 5.54%. Primark and Edinburgh Woollen Mill cancelled orders with Bangladeshi factories, and, by exporters' count, more than a quarter of the country's 4 million garment workers lost jobs or went unpaid ("Primark and Matalan Among Retailers," 2020). By 2023 it had fallen to 3.35% although growth slowed, so output and the headline rate do not move together (Kapoor, 2026). Growth slowed to 4.2% in 2024 amid political unrest, and unemployment rose again (Kapoor, 2026). In 2025, suppliers to Walmart put some orders on hold during U.S. tariff threats (Reuters, 2025).

## What Type of Unemployment Does Bangladesh Face?

Current unemployment is mainly structural, with cyclical swings on top. Joblessness reaches 13.54% among university graduates but only 1.25% among people with no formal education (TBS Report, 2025). The cause is a mismatch: manufacturing's share of employment fell from 14.4% in 2016–17 to about 11.5%, agriculture's rose to 44%, and under 2% of young workers hold formal paid jobs (Kapoor, 2026). Informality, at 84% of employment, means few people can afford to stay openly unemployed, so the rate understates slack (Kapoor, 2026; TBS Report, 2025).

In the short run, unemployment is cyclical: when firms expect weaker demand they hire less, and sticky wages keep pay from adjusting (Shapiro et al., 2023). Order cancellations, unrest, and tariff threats acted this way. Over the long run, unemployment settles at the natural rate, made of frictional and structural unemployment and shaped by skills, productivity, and institutions (Shapiro et al., 2023). Industry's productivity advantage over agriculture grew from 4.5 to 8 times between 2017 and 2022, yet workers moved toward farming (Kapoor, 2026). Short-run causes are demand shocks that reverse; long-run causes are job-structure problems that persist, so extra demand alone will not absorb graduates.

## How Is Inflation Measured, and What Has It Been?

Inflation held near 5.5–5.7% through 2021 (Table 1), climbed to 10.47% in 2024, and eased to 8.77% in 2025 (World Bank, n.d.). After Russia's invasion of Ukraine, higher global commodity prices and a weaker taka raised import prices (International Monetary Fund [IMF], 2023). BBS reported 8.27% for October 2026, with a 12-month average of 8.66% ("Inflation Dips," 2026).

BBS builds its Consumer Price Index (CPI) from separate urban and rural baskets, each with 383 items in 749 varieties priced in 154 markets. Weights come from a 2016–17 household expenditure survey, and prices are indexed to a 2021–22 base. The inflation rate is the change in the index divided by the earlier index, times 100, and the point-to-point rate compares a month with the same month a year earlier (BBS, 2026).

The CPI approximates the cost of living, the Producer Price Index tracks input costs, the GDP deflator covers all domestic output, and core inflation excludes volatile food and energy prices (Shapiro et al., 2023). BBS also publishes a Wage Rate Index for 63 occupations (BBS, 2026).

## What Are the Consequences, and Would a Developed Country Differ?

Inflation has outrun pay. In October 2026, the wage index rose 7.63% against 8.27% inflation ("Inflation Dips," 2026), and an ILO paper found real wages falling even as industrial productivity rose (Kapoor, 2026). Lacking formal protections, households take low-productivity work: own-account workers supplied 8.5 million of the 9.6 million jobs added between 2017 and 2022 (Kapoor, 2026). GDP growth dropped from 7.9% in 2019 to 3.5% in 2025 (World Bank, n.d.).

A developed country would read these figures differently. The United States pays unemployment benefits of about one-third of prior wages, usually for up to 26 weeks (Shapiro et al., 2023), so jobless workers can afford to search for a good match. Indexed programs such as Social Security benefits rise with the CPI (Shapiro et al., 2023), and the Federal Reserve targets 2% inflation (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, n.d.). Bangladeshi wages, by contrast, trailed prices. Developing economies' expectations are also less firmly anchored, and where inflation targeting is absent, exchange-rate swings pass through to prices more strongly (Ha et al., 2019). The natural rate for the United States was estimated near 4.6% in 2022, so a 3.6% rate would signal an economy above potential and 8–10% inflation would reflect excess demand (Shapiro et al., 2023). In Bangladesh, import prices drove inflation (IMF, 2023).
