# Economic Convergence Between Bangladesh and the United States, 2014–2024

Economic convergence occurs when poorer economies grow faster than rich ones and so narrow the income gap (Shapiro et al., 2023). This report tests that idea by comparing Bangladesh with the United States over 2014 to 2024. The development gap is large: in 2014, gross domestic product (GDP) per person in the United States was 48 times the Bangladeshi figure. Unless stated otherwise, GDP per capita is measured in constant 2015 U.S. dollars (World Bank, n.d.).

## Has Bangladesh Experienced Economic Expansion?

Yes. Bangladesh's GDP per capita rose from $1,159 in 2014 to $1,941 in 2024, a gain of 67%, or about 5.3% a year. The United States moved from $55,665 to $66,857, a gain of 20%, or about 1.8% a year (World Bank, n.d.). The American advantage therefore shrank from 48 times to 34 times, and Bangladesh reached 2.9% of the U.S. level, up from 2.1%. Measured in current international dollars, which adjust for price differences, Bangladesh's income grew 143% and climbed from 7.2% to 11.2% of the U.S. figure (World Bank, n.d.). Bangladesh also outgrew the United States in each of the 10 years from 2015 to 2024, including 2020, when its per capita output grew 2.6% while the U.S. figure fell 2.5%.

The expansion was uneven. Annual growth held between 5.6% and 7.0% from 2015 to 2019, recovered to about 6% in 2021 and 2022, then slowed to 4.5% in 2023 and 3.0% in 2024. Other indicators show strain: Bangladesh's inflation reached 10.5% in 2024, against 3.0% in the United States, and its trade deficit equaled 5.9% of GDP, against 3.1%. Unemployment was 3.6%, below the American 4.0%, but that rate may not capture underemployment, since nearly nine in 10 workers are informal (World Bank, n.d., 2026).

The convergence is also relative, not absolute. The dollar gap between the two countries widened from $54,506 to $64,915. This fits the textbook point that even fast growth from a low base takes decades to close a large gap (Shapiro et al., 2023). If the 2014–2024 growth rates continued, Bangladesh would need about 86 years to reach half of U.S. income per person (author's calculation from World Bank, n.d.).

## What Factors Contributed to Convergence?

Investment is the first factor. Gross capital formation was 30.7% of Bangladesh's GDP in 2024 against 21.4% in the United States, and it stayed above 28% all decade (World Bank, n.d.). Diminishing returns imply that added capital raises output more where capital is scarce (Shapiro et al., 2023). Infrastructure investment helped, and electricity access is now universal (World Bank, 2023).

Export manufacturing is the second factor. Ready-made garments supply 82% of exports and moved many workers into factory jobs (World Bank, 2026). The World Bank (2025) tied the slowdown from 5.8% growth in fiscal year 2023 to 4.2% in fiscal year 2024 to weaker exports and low investment. Exports also fell from 19.0% of GDP in 2014 to 10.5% in 2024, so they grew more slowly than the economy (World Bank, n.d.). A World Bank project that supported non-garment exporters created nearly 180,000 jobs, which shows diversification is already under way (World Bank, 2026).

Human capital and technology form the third factor. From 2014 to 2022, adult literacy rose from 61.1% to 79.0%. From 2014 to 2024, tertiary enrollment rose from 13.6% to 23.7%, and internet use from 9.1% to 53.4% (World Bank, n.d.). Girls now match boys in secondary enrollment, and female labor force participation rose from 36% to 43% in six years (World Bank, 2023). Gerschenkron's "advantages of backwardness" apply here, since a poorer country can adopt existing technology instead of inventing it (Shapiro et al., 2023). The gap remains large, however. Bangladesh spent 2.0% of GDP on education in 2024, while the United States spent 5.4% in 2021, its latest figure (World Bank, n.d.).

Macroeconomic policy is the fourth factor. The World Bank (2025) expects Bangladeshi inflation to ease through tight monetary policy, fiscal consolidation, and looser food import restrictions, although inflation was still 10.5% in 2024. The United States raised its federal funds target range to 5.25–5.50% in July 2023, after inflation reached 8.0% in 2022 (Federal Reserve, 2023; World Bank, n.d.).

## What Are the Implications of Convergence?

For Bangladeshi citizens, well-being has improved. Life expectancy rose from 70.0 to 74.9 years, so the gap with the United States narrowed from 8.8 years to 4.0, as American life expectancy barely moved, from 78.8 to 78.9 (World Bank, n.d.). Extreme poverty, measured at $2.15 a day, fell from 9.0% in 2016 to 5.0% in 2022 (World Bank, 2023). The Gini index, a measure of income inequality, moved from 32.4 to 30.9 between 2016 and 2022, well below the American 41.8 in 2024 (World Bank, n.d.). Growth therefore has not been captured by a narrow top group, at least in survey data. Quality of life still lags. Three in five workers hold vulnerable jobs, and education spending is low (World Bank, n.d., 2026).

For American citizens, the indicators show no cost from Bangladesh's catch-up. U.S. income per person rose 20%, and unemployment fell from 6.2% to 4.0%. The problems visible in the American data are domestic: inequality stayed close to a Gini of 41 throughout the decade, life expectancy stagnated, and the 2022 inflation spike eroded purchasing power (World Bank, n.d.). Bangladesh's catch-up also depends on outside demand, because a concentrated export base relies on wealthier markets (World Bank, 2025, 2026).

Overall, the evidence supports relative convergence: Bangladesh outgrew the United States every year and narrowed gaps in income, life expectancy, and inequality. One decade and two countries cannot establish causes, and slowing growth with double-digit inflation shows the process is fragile.
