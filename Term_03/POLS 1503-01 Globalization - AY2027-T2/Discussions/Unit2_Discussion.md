# Unit 2 Discussion: The IMF, the World Bank, and the Millennium Development Goals

The IMF and the World Bank both date to the 1944 Bretton Woods Conference, where forty-four allied nations tried to prevent a repeat of the currency warfare that helped bring on the Great Depression (Council on Foreign Relations [CFR], 2023). The IMF was designed as a watchdog of exchange rates and international payments. It has three core functions: surveillance of national and global economies, technical assistance, and lending to members with balance-of-payments problems (CFR, 2023). Members fund it through quotas based on their size. Loans carry conditions such as balancing budgets, removing subsidies, and privatizing state enterprises (CFR, 2023).

Each of these features draws criticism. Joseph Stiglitz argued that austerity, high interest rates, and privatization often hurt the economies they were meant to repair, although defenders note that countries usually call the IMF only after a crisis has begun, and nobody can know what the alternative would have looked like (CFR, 2023). Lending and surveillance have also drifted. Nordquist and Katz (2024) count 13 lending facilities and note that the IMF now comments on topics from cryptocurrency to artificial intelligence, where its expertise is unclear. They add that less than 1 percent of the $100 billion that rich countries pledged to pass on after the 2021 Special Drawing Rights allocation appears to have been provided. The quota system raises a governance concern too, since emerging economies hold smaller voting shares than the United States and Europe, and the managing director has long been European (CFR, 2023).

The World Bank Group has five institutions, and two of them, the IBRD and the IDA, are what people usually mean by "the World Bank" (Masters et al., 2023). The IBRD lends to middle-income and creditworthy low-income countries, while the IDA offers interest-free credits and grants to the poorest. Where the IMF fights short-term crises, the Bank handles longer-term development and poverty reduction. Policy authority sits with a board of governors, and by tradition an American leads the Bank. The United States holds double the voting share of Japan, the next largest shareholder (Masters et al., 2023).

Nordquist and Katz (2024) quote the Bank's own Independent Evaluation Group saying it has "too many priorities" and weak accountability for results, so the Bank is mostly interested in making new loans. Its roughly $2 billion a year in lending to China, which meets the graduation criteria, is their main example of poor prioritization. They also note a $5 million settlement paid to Honduran farmers over a Bank-funded project. Masters et al. (2023) add that Easterly called the plan to end poverty "utopian social engineering," and that rivals such as the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank grew partly from frustration with the Bank's terms. I think this matters because the World Trade Organization (WTO, 2023) reports that crises since 2008 have made many countries more sceptical of globalization and the multilateral trading system, so institutions that cannot show results lose credibility.

The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) grew out of world leaders' 2000 pledge to free people from extreme poverty and became eight goals for 2015, from ending poverty and hunger to universal primary education, gender equality, health, environmental sustainability, and a global partnership for development (United Nations, 2015, p. 3). Measurable targets made progress trackable, and the results were real. Extreme poverty fell from 1.9 billion people in 1990 to 836 million in 2015, and out-of-school primary-age children dropped from 100 million in 2000 to 57 million in 2015 (United Nations, 2015, p. 4).

Several improvements follow from the UN's own report. Progress was uneven, and children from the poorest households were four times as likely to be out of school as those from the richest (United Nations, 2015, p. 8). Better data would help, because a World Bank study found that about half of 155 countries lack adequate data to monitor poverty, so the poorest often remain invisible (United Nations, 2015, p. 10). Conflict and climate change also threatened gains, and 825 million people still lived in extreme poverty in 2015 (United Nations, 2015, pp. 8, 22). Trade could help as well, but the WTO (2023) notes that many least developed countries still struggle to take part in the global trading system. Finally, Nordquist and Katz (2024) show why clear priorities and accountability for results matter, and I would build both into any successor to the MDGs.
