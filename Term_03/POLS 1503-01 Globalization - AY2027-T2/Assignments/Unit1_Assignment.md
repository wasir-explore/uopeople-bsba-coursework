# Economic Globalization and Garment Workers in Bangladesh

Economic globalization is the growing interdependence of economies through cross-border trade, foreign direct investment, and global value chains (Alkharafi & Alsabah, 2025). I chose this thematic area from the article because Bangladesh, my region, shows its benefits and its costs unusually clearly. Ready-made garments (RMG) make up about 82% of the country's export revenue (International Labour Organization [ILO], 2023).

## How Globalization Changed People's Lives

According to Alkharafi and Alsabah (2025), economic globalization creates jobs in export industries, widens access to goods and services, and can raise wages and working conditions when multinational firms enter a market. Bangladesh's garment sector fits this description. It employs about 4 million workers, and in 2018 an estimated 60.5% of them were women. The typical worker is a 23-year-old woman who moved from a rural area (ILO, 2023). These figures suggest that export manufacturing opened wage work to young rural women on a large scale.

The same article warns that firms relocate production to places with lower wages, weaker labor regulation, and limited union protection, which in extreme cases leads to human rights violations (Alkharafi & Alsabah, 2025). Rana Plaza shows that risk. When the building collapsed near Dhaka on April 24, 2013, it killed 1,138 workers and injured more than 2,500 (ILO, 2021). The main causes were the authorities' failure to enforce building regulations and the owner's disregard of licensing rules, and weak worker voice made matters worse (ILO, 2021). Globalization therefore changed lives in two directions for the same group of workers. It gave many of them wages, and it tied those wages to factories that weren't always safe.

## The Conflict and the Proposed Solutions

The conflict pitted low-cost export production against workers' safety and rights. The article also notes that economic globalization can widen inequality in developing regions, because the gains often go to business owners and elites (Alkharafi & Alsabah, 2025). To respond, Alkharafi and Alsabah (2025) propose that policymakers in developing nations make sure trade agreements include labor protections, and that governments in developed nations invest in education and reskilling to offset job losses from offshoring. Bangladesh's response after 2013 followed the first idea. The government and private sector assessed 3,780 export-oriented garment factories for structural, electrical, and fire safety. The Labour Act was amended in 2013 to simplify union registration, and Better Work Bangladesh began in 2014 to improve factory compliance. By October 2015, more than $34 million had been paid to victims' families and injured workers, and in 2022 the government started a pilot employment injury scheme (ILO, 2023).

Progress remains incomplete. The ILO (2021) says not all factories meet the agreed standards, and workers still report anti-union discrimination and unfair labor practices (ILO, 2023).

## Avoiding Similar Conflicts in the Future

Four steps would help even if globalization keeps accelerating. First, labor protections should be built into trade agreements, as Alkharafi and Alsabah (2025) recommend for developing nations, and buyers' supply contracts should carry the same standards. Second, governments need the capacity to enforce those rules, and the ILO (2021) argues that stronger regulation, accountability, and worker voice make disasters like Rana Plaza preventable. Third, countries need adjustment policies for people who lose out. Goldberg et al. (2023) link the turn against trade around 2015 and 2016, including Brexit and new U.S. tariffs, to worries about displaced workers and communities left behind. Reskilling and support for those workers address a main source of that backlash. Fourth, diversification lowers dependence on one sector. A World Bank (2026) project helped non-garment exporters meet environmental, social, and quality standards, and it created nearly 180,000 jobs. Safety work should also reach other industries, since the ILO (2023) cites a fatal March 2023 explosion at the Seema Oxygen Plant as a sign that garment-sector progress has not carried over everywhere.

## How My Region Has Become More Globalized

World Bank (n.d.) data show the change. Merchandise exports grew from $1.7 billion in 1990 to $47.2 billion in 2024. Trade rose from 19.0% of gross domestic product (GDP) in 1990 to a peak of 48.1% in 2012. Internet use went from 0.07% of the population in 2000 to 53.4% in 2024, which links households to global information and markets. Remittances, the money migrants send home, rose from 2.5% of GDP in 1990 to 6.1% in 2024, after peaking at 10.6% in 2012. Together these figures show deeper links through goods, ideas, and labor.

## Seeing the Results Today

I can see the results today, with one qualification. The garment sector's 82% share of exports and its 4 million workers (ILO, 2023) make globalization part of everyday economic life, and more than half the population now uses the internet (World Bank, n.d.). The qualification is that trade has fallen from its 2012 peak to 26.8% of GDP in 2024, and exports of goods and services fell from 20.2% to 10.5% of GDP (World Bank, n.d.). This doesn't mean exports collapsed, because merchandise exports still rose from $25.1 billion in 2012 to $47.2 billion in 2024 (World Bank, n.d.). It also fits the wider picture in which globalization slowed after about 2015 without reversing, and countries have had different experiences (Goldberg et al., 2023). My region is therefore still globalized, but at a changed pace, and its heavy reliance on garments leaves it exposed to the conflicts discussed above.
